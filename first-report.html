What the car actually is

W166 — and 2012 was the first year of it. The third-generation M-Class replaced the W164 that ran from 2006 to 2011, and it was a genuinely new vehicle rather than a facelift: double-wishbone front suspension, multi-link rear, permanent 4MATIC, and electromechanical steering from launch rather than a hydraulic rack. It shares its bones with the X166 GL-Class. In 2015 it was facelifted and renamed GLE, which is why parts catalogues cover 2012 through 2019 as one family.

That generation boundary is the single most useful fact about this car, and it comes back later in a way that matters a great deal.

Engine and drivetrain

The M276 DE35 — a 3.5-litre direct-injection V6 making 302 hp at 6,500 rpm and 273 lb-ft at 3,500, driving all four wheels through the 7G-Tronic seven-speed. The M276 replaced the M272, and the replacement was aimed squarely at the two things the M272 was notorious for: a balance shaft gear that wore out, and a timing chain tensioner that struggled at high mileage. The M276's 60-degree vee angle removes the need for a balance shaft at all. If someone tells your friend that old Mercedes V6s eat their balance shafts, they are describing a different engine in a different M-Class, and it is worth knowing that before the first person says it.

What the M276 does have is a set of quieter, more manageable habits. Camshaft adjusters and secondary chain tensioners bleed down oil pressure as they age and announce it as a brief metallic rattle on a cold start. The upper timing covers and camshaft adjuster magnet seals weep oil. And the expensive version of that last one: oil wicking down the wiring harness from the camshaft sensor connectors and reaching the engine control module, which turns a seal job into something much larger. Direct injection also means carbon accumulates on the intake valves over time; at 89,000 miles that is approaching rather than overdue.

Real-world fuel economy: owners of the 4MATIC ML350 commonly report 16 to 19 mpg in mixed driving and low twenties on a long highway run. The EPA figures are 17 city, 22 highway, 19 combined.

Towing: the 2012 ML350 4MATIC is rated at 7,200 pounds with the factory towing package. Whether this particular car has the hitch is not established — the trailer hitch appears in the supplied document as a $550 option in a model catalogue, not as confirmed equipment. Look under the rear bumper for a receiver. Without one, it tows nothing at all.

What breaks and what it means

Three gaps in the record, in order of what they cost.

The transmission is first, and it is a gap rather than a problem. Eleven service records, 89,000 miles, fourteen years, and not one line mentions transmission fluid. The 7G-Tronic is a durable gearbox that dislikes old fluid, and what old fluid eventually produces is a conductor plate or a valve body — $600 to $1,200 and $1,500 to $2,500 respectively. A fluid and filter service is $450 to $750. Do it, and have the transmission and 4MATIC modules scanned at the same visit, because a scan is the only way to know whether this box is currently healthy or already logging faults nobody has looked at. Note that a generic OBD-II code reader will not tell you: it reads emissions codes and will walk straight past the transmission, the airbag module, the steering module and the suspension controller. This platform needs Mercedes-level diagnostics — a dealer's XENTRY, or an independent running a capable Mercedes-specific tool.

The rear brakes are second, and this is one of those details that only turns up when somebody actually reads the records rather than counting them. The single piece of brake work in this car's entire documented life is 10 February 2021, at Pep Boys, at 53,073 miles: front pads, front rotors, brake fluid flush. Fourteen years, and the rear axle has never appeared. Rear pads and rotors run $570 to $800, and the fluid is now over five years old, another $120 to $200. Measure the rear pads before assuming anything — they may be fine — but nobody has ever written down that they are.

Coolant is third. No record, ever, in fourteen South Florida years. Call it $200 to $400 including a pressure test, and understand that the pressure test is the real product: it is how you find a weeping thermostat housing or a tired hose in a driveway rather than on a highway.

Then the two things that might cost real money and might cost nothing at all. If the cold-start rattle is present, budget $1,200 to $2,500 for camshaft adjusters, solenoids and an upper timing cover reseal, and more again if the harness connectors are oily and the engine computer has been contaminated. Nobody has reported a rattle on this car, and the specialist who inspected it in May 2026 checked drivability and performance without raising one. And the suspension: the Dynamic Handling Package with AIRMATIC air springs and the Active Curve System was a $5,150 option in 2012. The supplied dimension data lists 19-inch wheels, and that package came with 20s, which points toward ordinary steel springs — but the option list in the same document is a catalogue of what could have been ordered, not a record of what was. Settle it by looking under the car. If it does turn out to be air and a corner is failing, a strut is $900 to $1,600 and a compressor $600 to $1,100, and this report would carry a harder badge on the next assessment.

The 2020 collision is the one thing in this car's history that no document can finish for you. On 29 May 2020 a police report was filed in Hollywood, Florida: rear impact with another vehicle, moderate damage, report number 332005080312. Three days later a damage report added left side, right side, rear and bumper. Two structural checks ran across the two documents and both returned nothing found — that is a result and it is worth having, but what it means precisely is that no structural record reached those providers, not that anyone verified the rear structure is straight. Airbag deployment status is not in either document. No repair invoice is in either document.

Six years on, the questions are ordinary body-shop questions and any decent shop will answer them in twenty minutes. Do the panel gaps at the tailgate and both rear quarters match side to side. Does a paint depth gauge read anything wild across the rear third of the car. Is there overspray in the spare wheel well, on the rear seat belt anchors, or on the underside of the tailgate seal. Do the tail light seals sit flush. A moderate rear impact that was repaired properly is a footnote on a fourteen-year-old car. One that was repaired cheaply shows itself in exactly those places.

The second flag is about the future rather than the past, and it is the most actionable sentence in this report. This car spent fourteen years registered in Hallandale, Hollywood and Islamorada, Florida, and its underbody has never seen road salt. It is now registered in Ontario. That untouched underbody is a genuine asset and it is about to start being spent. The cheapest and highest-return thing anyone will ever do for this vehicle is get it oil-sprayed or rust-proofed before the first winter, and then again every autumn. It costs a couple of hundred dollars and it is the difference between this car being worth something in five years and not.

Third, the recall list — and this is where the generation boundary earns its keep. The supplied documents carry seven recall entries, and at least two of them do not belong to this vehicle. One, campaign 12V557000, concerns a fuel filter flange, and its own text names the S400, S550, S63 AMG, C300, E350, GLK350, CL550 and CL63; there is no M-Class anywhere in it, and it appears twice in the same document. The other is the one most likely to frighten an owner who reads it cold: the 2022 brake booster corrosion campaign, the one that came with a stop-driving advisory. NHTSA's own acknowledgment letter for campaign 22V-315 lists the affected M-Class as "MERCEDES BENZ/ML350/2006-2011". That is the W164. This is a 2012 W166. The headline range reads 2006–2012 because the R-Class and GL-Class ran through 2012, not because the 2012 ML350 is in the population.

Of the remainder: the 2018 Active Curve System campaign applies only to vehicles actually fitted with ACS, which is the same unresolved Dynamic Handling Package discussed above — settle the suspension question and this one settles with it. The 2018 steering campaign covers cars sold or registered in heavy road-salt provinces and names Quebec, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland & Labrador; Ontario is not on that list and this car spent its life in Florida, though it is now a Canadian vehicle and worth checking anyway. The 2012 floor mat campaign and the 2017 airbag control unit campaign are both plausible for the platform.

None of that is confirmation of anything, in either direction. A recall list inside a history report is a loose match and carries no completion status whatsoever. Run the VIN through Transport Canada now that the car is registered in Ontario, and through NHTSA for its fourteen American years. Both are free and both take a minute, and they are the only sources that will tell you what is genuinely open against this specific vehicle.

Driveway inspection

Start with the engine, cold, before anything else. Pull the dipstick before starting: amber or light brown is right, black and gritty is neglect, and anything creamy on the oil filler cap underside means stop. Then look at the top front of each cylinder head, at the small silver covers, for oil film — and pull the camshaft sensor connectors and check inside them for oil, because that is the path to the engine computer. Start it and listen to the first five seconds. Watch the exhaust on the cold start: blue means oil, persistent white beyond thirty seconds means coolant.

Then the platform-specific checks. Look behind the right rear wheel and under the load floor for an air compressor and reservoir, and check the console for a height-adjustment switch — that answers the suspension question. Park it on flat ground and check all four corners sit level, then bounce each corner and confirm it settles in one rebound. Look under the rear bumper for a factory trailer hitch receiver. Open the coolant reservoir cold and look at the colour: clean and correct is right, brown, rusty or oily is not. Measure the rear pads through the wheels. Pull the floor mats and press the carpet, front and rear, feeling for damp — on a South Florida car with a panorama roof the culprits are blocked sunroof drains and the A/C condensate drain, and fourteen years is long enough for both. Test every window up and down. Confirm how many working keys came with the car, because Mercedes keys are not cheap to replace. And walk the rear third of the car slowly in good light doing the panel gap and paint checks described above.
